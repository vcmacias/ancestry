A documented descent, Jalisco · Zacatecas · Arizona · Kansas · Chihuahua

Six generations of the Macías family, from a rancho called Guadalupe to the Zaragoza bridge

Every name and date below stands on a primary document: a parish register, a civil act, a birth certificate, a border card. Where the records disagree, the page says so. The line now runs back to a baptism in May 1788, and stops there for a reason the last section explains.

The descent

Read downward. The line on the left is the direct descent. Everything set beside it is family standing off the line.

Fifth great-grandparents · the earliest names

Manuel Macías and María Felipa González

Named on two documents and no more: their son's marriage act of 1813, and a baptism of 28 May 1788 which places a couple of these same two names at Guadalupe, a rancho of the Teocaltiche jurisdiction, and records their children as mulato. They did not marry at Teocaltiche and they were not from there. Where they came from is the open question of this page.

Known children: José Antonio (about 1787) · José María, baptised 28 May 1788, born fourteen days earlier

Fourth great-grandparents

José Antonio Macías and Matiana Olmos

Married at Teocaltiche on 14 January 1813, he twenty-five and she twenty-two, both living at los Cuervos. The clerk calls him vecino, a resident, never originario, a native, and leaves the caste column beside his name blank while filling it in for his bride and for the couple married just above them. By 1833 the family had moved to Buenavista. He was dead before February 1854, when his son's marriage register calls him el finado.

Matiana's parents

Lorenzo de Olmos and María López

A local Teocaltiche family, which is how we know José Antonio married into the parish rather than out of it. Lorenzo was already difunto by the wedding in 1813.

Third great-grandparents

Luis Macías Olmos and Daría Acosta Martínez

Luis, born 1833 or 1834 at Buenavista, a jornalero, died at two in the morning on 17 July 1893 at Teocaltiche. Daría, born about 1835, outlived him by fifteen years and died at Teocaltiche on 20 July 1908.

Married 4 February 1854, Nuestra Señora de los Dolores, Teocaltiche. He was twenty, she was eighteen.

Their children: María Encarnación (1863) · Sotero (about 1864) · Juana (1869) · María Ascención (1872) · José Teodosio · Paula · Antonio · María Plásida

And on the other sides

Gervasio Acosta and Trinidad Martínez

Named on their daughter’s marriage act in 1854, which gives Daría as eighteen. A baptism at La Santísima Trinidad in Villa Hidalgo in 1831 names the same two parents, and would put the family in the same parish where Ascensión Cordero was christened fifty-five years later — but it would also make Daría twenty-two at her wedding, so it may belong to another woman of the same name.

Juan Ruiz and Fortunata

Parents of Agustina Ruiz. Her surname is written Islas in one grandson's baptism and Aguero in another.

Second great-grandparents

Sotero Macías Acosta and Agustina Ruiz

Sotero was born about 1864 and died at twenty-nine, on the night of 6 July 1893, of fríos viciosos. His mother Daría walked to the civil registry the next morning to report it. Agustina was left a widow with an infant.

Their children: Bernardino (1881) · Maximiano (1883) · Maximino (died 1884) · Porfiria (1885) · Francisca · María · Rafaela · Perfecta · Ambrosio (1890, died 1893) · María (1891, died 1961) · José Manuel (1892)

And on Ascensión's side

Petronilo Cordero and Antonia Arriaga

Of Paso de Sotos, Jalisco. Their daughter Ascensión was born 3 June 1886 and registered at Teocaltiche two days later.

Great-grandparents

Manuel Macías Ruiz and Ascensión Cordero

Manuel was born at eight in the morning on 26 December 1892 at La Ciénega, in the Nochistlán parish, and baptised there on 11 February 1893. For the rest of his life he told American officials he was born on the twenty-fourth. He was a copper miner at Morenci, never naturalised, and carried a burn scar on his right jaw, noted on his border cards, which is how two of them were proved to be the same man. He died in 1965.

Two brothers, one wife

Ascensión Cordero married Bernardino Macías at Morenci in 1914, twice in seven weeks: before a justice of the peace on 5 March, and before a Catholic pastor on 22 April. Bernardino was Manuel's older brother, born at Teocaltiche on 19 May 1881 to the same parents.

Bernardino went north first. Manuel followed him in 1915. After Bernardino was gone, Manuel married his brother's widow and raised his children. Because both men were Macías, no family tree ever caught the seam.

Ascensión's six children: Candelaria (1915) and Ascensión Petronilo (1918) and Ester (about 1920) were Bernardino's, and are marked step-children to Manuel on the 1930 census. Pedra was born in 1916 and died in 1917. Carmen (1925) and Manuel Jr. (1928) were Manuel's own, and the census marks them plainly as his sons.

Grandparents

Carmen Cordero Macías and Guadalupe Amézquita

Carmen was born 9 April 1925 in Kansas, at a place he himself called “Laines, Kansas” — no such town exists, and it is most likely Lyons, in Rice County, heard in Spanish and written down by ear. He died in 1998. He registered for the draft at Fort Hancock, Texas, in April 1943, naming his father Manuel as his contact. Lupe was born in 1920, in New Mexico, and the family had moved to Texas by the time her brother Antonio was born a few years later. The 1940 census finds her at Ysleta, El Paso County, aged twenty and single, in the household of her widowed mother Isabel, a seamstress; her grandmother Emilia, aged seventy-five, was living with them. Her father Marcelino was already dead. She lived until 2009. The 1944 marriage act gives her birthplace as “Calabad, N.M.”, which is very likely Carlsbad, though no record yet says so outright.

Married 8 November 1944 at Zaragoza, Chihuahua. Both were living at Ysleta, Texas, and crossed the river to marry.

The wall at 1788

The line ends on one page of a Teocaltiche baptism register, and the reason it ends there is not that the record is missing. It is that the family had only just arrived.

En Theoc.e en veinte y ocho de Mayo de ochenta y ocho, Yo el Br. D.n Fernando Mart.z baup.é solednem.te y puse los santo olios á Jose Maria, mulato, de Guadalupe, de catorce dias de nacida, h.l. de Man.l Macias y de Maria Felipa Gonz., P.s Jose Calisto Romo… Bautismos 1787–1795 · DGS 004739969 img 40 · left leaf, fourth entry

Four facts in one sentence: the date, the rancho, the caste, and the parents. It is the only document that describes this couple rather than merely naming them, and it was found by turning pages, because that film appears in no index and no full-text corpus.

What the register says about them, and what it does not

  • •They were at Guadalupe, a rancho of the Teocaltiche jurisdiction, in May 1788, and their children were written mulato. Twenty-five years later their son was living at los Cuervos, a different rancho in the same jurisdiction.
  • •Those two acts are joined only by the parents' names. Teocaltiche had three or four different women called Felipa González inside five years, one of them a Doña, and Manuel Macías is a common name, so the caste written on the 1788 page is not yet certain to be this family's. José Antonio's own baptism would settle it, and it should lie in the first forty images of the same film.
  • •On his 1813 marriage act the clerk fills in the caste for the bride and for the couple married directly above, and leaves the space beside José Antonio empty. He is called a resident of los Cuervos, never a native of anywhere.

Manuel and Felipa did not marry at Teocaltiche, and neither of them died there. Their marriage is not in that parish's registers, which survive unbroken from 1753 to 1804, nor in any indexed marriage in Mexico for the years they must have married. They appear in the parish for the first time as parents, already with children, already settled on a rancho. Everything about the record says the same thing: they came from somewhere else, and they arrived shortly before 1788. Finding that somewhere is the work that remains.

Where they lived, and why they moved

For four generations the family stayed inside a thirty-mile circle of Los Altos. Then, in one generation, they went north to Arizona, on to Kansas, back to Arizona, and were pushed into Mexico again.

Dots in green are places in Mexico, in copper the United States. The four generations at Los Altos lived inside a circle thirty miles across.

Seven weeks

July to August 1893

A fever went through the first ward of Teocaltiche and took three generations of Macías men. This is why the next generation left.

20 Aug

Manuel never knew his father. Sixteen years later his brother Bernardino left for the copper camps of Arizona, and in 1915 Manuel followed him.

What the register says killed them

Sotero died of fríos viciosos, literally “vicious chills”, the phrase Mexican registrars used through the nineteenth century for the violent recurring fevers now understood as malarial. Luis’s cause is written only as fiebre. Neither act uses the word epidemic.

Why July

Both men died in the first weeks of the summer rains, which is when fever carried by standing water peaked in the barrancas of Los Altos. Both were jornaleros, day labourers working outdoors in it. Ambrosio, aged two, died six weeks later at Nochistlán, sixty miles away.

Daría did the reporting

She was fifty. On 7 July she walked to the civil registry at ten in the morning and reported the death of her son. Eleven days later she returned to the same office, at the same hour, and reported her husband’s. On the second act the clerk wrote her status as viuda. She lived another fifteen years.

What it set in motion

In seven weeks the family lost its grandfather, its father and its youngest child, and Agustina was widowed with an infant and several older children. Three deaths in one household in one summer is not ordinary mortality. It is also the most likely reason that, when the next generation came of age, they left for Arizona rather than stay.

Kansas

about 1920 to about 1926

Almost certainly railroad section work on the Santa Fe line, the other great draw for Mexican labour in those years. The family is absent from both the 1920 federal census and the 1925 state census, which is what usually happened to section-camp households.

Still open

Eight things this page cannot yet tell you, in order of how much they would change it.

Where Manuel and Felipa came from

The largest question on the page, and the one that would add the most. They married somewhere between about 1780 and 1786, and it was not Teocaltiche. Whichever parish that was, its registers hold their marriage, their parents' names, and the generation before them.

José Antonio's baptism

He was twenty-five in January 1813, so born about 1787, and the 1788 baptism of his brother shows the family in the parish by May of that year. His own baptism has never been found, in Teocaltiche or anywhere else.

Where in New Mexico Lupe was born

The 1940 census gives only New Mexico, with no town. Carlsbad is an assumption, resting on the 1944 marriage act, where a Mexican clerk wrote her birthplace as “Calabad, N.M.” A New Mexico birth record from 1920 would settle it, and her Texas-born siblings’ records would show how fast the family crossed.

Where Ortega comes from

Ascensión's own surname was Cordero and her mother was an Arriaga. No document yet explains the Ortega she used for forty years.

Manuel's death record

The family date is 1 June 1965 at Ciudad Juárez. The Juárez register for 31 May to 7 June was read page by page and he is not in it.

Which county in Kansas

“Laines” comes from Carmen himself, twice: on his draft card in 1943 and at his wedding in 1944. But no Kansas town of that name exists, and the family appears in neither the 1920 federal census nor the 1925 state census.

He was two when the family left Kansas, so he never knew the place. It is a name heard from his parents in Spanish and written down by ear in west Texas fifteen years later. On that reading the likeliest candidate is Lyons, in Rice County: LY-onz is close to Laines, and Lyons was one of only three Kansas salt-mining towns that drew Mexican labour in those years, on the Santa Fe line the family would have followed. A Rice County household that looked promising turned out to be a different family, so nothing confirms it. The theory stands on the sound of the word and on where the work was.

When Ascensión died

She was alive at Zaragoza in November 1944, listed with her own address on her son's marriage act. Nothing after that.

What happened to Bernardino

He is in four Arizona documents between 1914 and 1918 and then nothing. No death record has been found in Arizona, Kansas or Mexico.
